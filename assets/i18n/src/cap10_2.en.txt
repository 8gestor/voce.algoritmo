## c10-2-002
War Economy:<br>The Engineering of Bitcoin and Monero

## c10-2-003
Bitcoin is, above all, a computer science invention that attacked an old problem of exchange: <strong>Trustless Consensus</strong>. For the first time, it's possible to transfer value without asking permission from a central intermediary. Plenty of people do use it as a speculative investment, and the price is extremely volatile; here we're interested in another face: a censorship-resistant network, relevant in a world of CBDCs. "Resistant" doesn't mean "immune": miners, exchanges and the on-ramp and off-ramp services (the points where money turns into local currency) can exclude, filter or track transactions.

## c10-2-004
<span class="thesis-header">// THE ENERGY WALL (PROOF OF WORK)</span> "Bitcoin spends energy on purpose. This is called <strong>Unforgeable Costliness</strong>. To rewrite the transaction history and censor a payment, an attacker would need to control more than half of the network's computing power (the so-called 51% attack), which would cost an enormous amount in hardware and electricity. The physical cost is the shield; but it comes at a price: the network consumes electricity on the scale of small countries (estimates on the order of 100 to 200 TWh a year), a real tension with Chapter 09."

## c10-2-008
The Privacy War: Bitcoin vs. Monero

## c10-2-009
Bitcoin has a flaw for resistance: it's transparent. Everyone can see where the money came from. For hostile regimes, we need obfuscation.

## c10-2-010
<div class="privacy-col" style="border-top:3px solid var(--c-btc)"> <strong style="color:var(--c-btc)">BITCOIN (Pseudonymous)</strong> <p style="font-size:0.8rem">Public ledger. If the police know your address, they know your whole history. Risk of "dirty" coins being rejected.</p> </div> <div class="privacy-col" style="border-top:3px solid var(--c-xmr)"> <strong style="color:var(--c-xmr)">MONERO (Anonymous)</strong> <p style="font-size:0.8rem">Uses <em>Ring Signatures</em>, stealth addresses and confidential transactions to hide sender, recipient and amount, by default. It's private digital cash, but "private" doesn't mean "invisible": several exchanges delisted Monero under regulatory pressure (Binance, in 2024), which makes it harder to convert into local currency.</p> </div>

## c10-2-011
Real Cases

## c10-2-012
<strong>Ukraine (2022):</strong> After the Russian invasion, the government and Ukrainian NGOs received tens of millions of dollars in crypto donations within a few days (according to blockchain analytics firms), at a speed that the convulsing banking system couldn't offer.<br> <strong>Afghanistan:</strong> There are reports of women and activists who used cryptocurrencies to store and receive funds out of reach of the Taliban-controlled banking system (press reports, hard to verify independently).<br> <strong>Nigeria:</strong> In 2020, the Feminist Coalition, which funded part of the #EndSARS protests, began receiving donations in Bitcoin after the central bank ordered the freezing of supporters' and organizers' accounts.

## c10-2-013
Financial Privacy, Legal Risk and Tensions

## c10-2-014
The argument for self-custody gained strength in November 2022, when FTX collapsed: customers who left their coins on the exchange didn't control the keys, the judge in the case estimated their losses at about 8 billion dollars, and the founder, Sam Bankman-Fried, was sentenced in 2023 to 25 years in prison.

## c10-2-015
An important update: the <strong>Samourai</strong> wallet, cited in earlier editions of this chapter, has been shut down. In 2024, its founders were arrested and the website was seized; in 2025, they were sentenced to five and four years in prison for operating an unlicensed transaction-mixing service (the indictment spoke of more than 237 million dollars in illicit funds moved). The case is a warning: financial privacy tools face real legal risk, and whoever builds or uses them needs to know the rules of their country.

## c10-2-016
Bitcoin is censorship-resistant at the protocol level, but there are choke points: regulated services that comply with sanctions lists (such as the US OFAC list), exchanges with identity verification, and mining pools. On Ethereum, more than half of blocks were at one point produced by sanction-compliant intermediaries after the 2022 change. Real resistance depends on using the network non-custodially and on having on-ramps and off-ramps that you control.

## c10-2-017
There is also a tension with Chapter 09: Bitcoin consumes a lot of energy. Some argue that this is the price of security, and others point out that alternatives, like proof of stake, use far less. And an alternative financial system isn't neutral: it concentrates wealth, attracts fraud and shifts risk onto the user (estimates point to millions of bitcoins lost forever through misplaced keys). For most people, practical financial sovereignty starts closer to home: cash for small purchases, accounts at different institutions, and knowing your own rights.
