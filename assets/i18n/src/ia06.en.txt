## ia06-002
The Race:<br>Chips, Energy and Sovereignty

## ia06-003
On January 27, 2025, Nvidia lost about 589 billion dollars in market value in a single day, the biggest daily drop ever recorded by a company, and its shares fell almost 17%. The reason was a Chinese startup. DeepSeek released a model that, according to the company itself, had been trained on weaker chips and for less than 6 million dollars. All of a sudden, investors began to doubt that the AI race would be won only by whoever spent the most.

## ia06-004
The doubt didn't last, and spending kept growing. By the <em>Financial Times</em>'s count, Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon and Meta are expected to invest about 725 billion dollars together in 2026, against about 410 billion in 2025. <span class="selo selo-estimativa">estimate</span> Other tallies range between 630 and 740 billion, and those figures are company projections, not money already spent, and they include construction that isn't always about AI. In any version, it's the money of a country, in the hands of four companies.

## ia06-005
This chapter looks at that race from the angle of power. Who has the chips, who has the energy, who writes the rules and, in the middle of all that, what Brazil can do.

## ia06-006
<span class="thesis-header">// The Race Has Three Bottlenecks</span> Training and running a cutting-edge AI depends on three scarce things: advanced chips, abundant electricity and permission to operate. Whoever controls a bottleneck controls everyone else's speed. That's why the technology contest has turned into industrial policy, diplomacy and, more and more, national defense.

There's also a question the industry prefers to postpone. If the investments are so huge, will the return come? In October 2025, the Bank of England and the IMF warned that market valuations resembled the peak of the dot-com bubble, and the Bank of England cited what it called an "AI spending frenzy and circular funding," in which companies invest in each other and buy from each other. <span class="selo selo-hipotese">hypothesis</span> Whether there's a bubble or not, nobody knows. ## ia06-013
And Brazil?

## ia06-014
Brazil has a plan. The Brazilian Artificial Intelligence Plan calls for investments of 23 billion reais between 2024 and 2028, and in August 2026 the government announced an expansion of the plan with supercomputers and a national cloud. The amounts and the number of machines vary across reports: one speaks of a supercomputer of about 1 billion reais in Rio Grande do Norte, another of a 1.8-billion machine with about 5,000 GPUs, and a third of more than 2 billion for two supercomputers. According to the MCTI secretary, 30% to 35% of the plan's actions have already been carried out or are under way.

## ia06-015
In September 2026, the government signed into law Redata, a special regime that suspends federal taxes, such as the import tax, PIS/Cofins and IPI, on equipment purchases for data centers. Among the conditions, at least 10% of installed capacity must be available to the Brazilian market. According to reports, the goal is to reduce dependence on infrastructure abroad, since about 60% of the country's digital workloads run on services from outside. Redata attracts investment, but it also raises questions about water, energy and who benefits, the same ones as in Chapter 17. <span class="selo selo-hipotese">hypothesis</span>

## ia06-016
On regulation, Bill 2338 remains stalled in the Chamber of Deputies. In August 2026, it was still in a special committee, with no date for a vote, and the election year lowers the chance of progress. The practical consequence is that Brazil attracts data centers and buys supercomputers without yet having a general law saying how AI can be used, audited and contested.

## ia06-017
The Honest Counterpoint

## ia06-018
Sovereignty isn't self-sufficiency. No country makes all the chips, writes all the models and generates all the energy AI consumes, and trying to would be expensive and probably pointless. The useful question is a different one: in each layer, is there an alternative if the supplier changes its mind. A Brazilian model, like Sabiá, helps, but if it runs on chips and clouds from abroad, sovereignty is partial. The size of the margin matters more than the flag.

## ia06-019
<span class="thesis-header">// Sovereignty in Layers</span> Sovereignty in AI isn't having a model with the country's flag on it. It's having <strong>room to say no in each layer</strong>: chips, energy, cloud, data and rules. Faced with any announcement, the citizen's question is which layer it covers and which one we still depend on someone else for.
