## c10-1-002
Digital Fortress:<br>Zero Trust and Tactical Hardening

## c10-1-003
Most security advice ("install an antivirus") is based on an obsolete model. Today, the attack comes from mass-collection algorithms and zero-day flaws. To survive, we adopt the <strong>Zero Trust</strong> model: assume your network is already compromised, that your provider is hostile and that the attack is inevitable. The goal isn't to keep them out, it's to limit the damage through <strong>Hardening</strong>.

## c10-1-004
The Colonial Pipeline case shows the principle in practice. In May 2021, a ransomware attack shut down a fuel pipeline in the US. According to the CEO, the intruders got in through an old VPN, with no multi-factor authentication and unknown to the IT team, using a compromised password.

## c10-1-005
The Mathematics of Secrecy (E2EE)

## c10-1-006
When you use Signal or WhatsApp, a miracle of mathematics takes place: <strong>public-key (asymmetric) cryptography</strong>. Your phone generates two keys: a Public one (which locks the safe) and a Private one (which unlocks it). If I want to send you a message, I use your public key. Neither I, nor the government, nor the company can open it afterward. The security lies in mathematical problems believed to be unfeasible to solve in practice (like the discrete logarithm on elliptic curves, used by modern messengers such as Signal, and, in older systems, the factoring of gigantic prime numbers), and not in the company's "goodness."

The Submarine Theory: Compartmentalization

## c10-1-009
<span class="thesis-header">// ISOLATION BY DESIGN</span> "Common operating systems are monolithic: if a virus gets in through the browser, it accesses everything. Secure systems like <strong>Qubes OS</strong> work like a submarine with watertight compartments. Each app runs in an isolated virtual machine. ## c10-1-014
Threat Model, Metadata and the Backdoor War

## c10-1-015
Security only makes sense against a <strong>threat model</strong>: who do you want to protect yourself from, what's at stake and at what cost? For an ordinary person, the essentials (automatic updates, unique passwords in a manager, two-factor authentication, end-to-end encryption) already block the overwhelming majority of risks; for a journalist threatened by a state, the calculation is different. The "tier" ranking above is an opinion about robustness, not a universal recommendation: an "S tier" system used badly is less secure than an ordinary system used well. Guides like the EFF's <em>Surveillance Self-Defense</em> help you design your own model.

## c10-1-016
Encryption protects the <em>content</em>, not the <em>metadata</em> (with whom, when, from where). The former director of the NSA and the CIA Michael Hayden said, in 2014: "We kill people based on metadata." Messengers differ a lot on this: Signal keeps almost no metadata; WhatsApp has end-to-end encryption on messages, but belongs to a company whose business model depends on data.

## c10-1-017
Encryption is also a legislative battlefield. Proposals like the "Chat Control" in the European Union seek message scanning, and in 2025 the British government demanded access to Apple's encrypted data, and Apple pulled Advanced Data Protection for users in the UK. Experts warn: there's no such thing as a "backdoor only for the good guys." And encryption has to evolve: Signal adopted, in 2023, a protocol (PQXDH) designed to resist future quantum computers.
