Introduction:<br>The Predictive Mirror

## in-003
<span style="display:block; margin-bottom:10px; font-weight:bold; color:var(--c-red); text-transform:uppercase; letter-spacing:1px;">⚠ ACTIVE SURVEILLANCE // REC...</span> This text is not fiction. While your eyes move across these lines, it is likely that the page, the app or the browser is recording how you read: how long you spend on each paragraph, how far you scroll, where you are accessing it from. That data feeds a profile that tries to predict what you will want, buy or feel, and in certain contexts it gets it right more often than you would like.

## in-004
Stop for a second. Feel the weight of the device in your hand and the screen lighting up your face. Now ask yourself with brutal honesty: when was the last time you made a decision that was entirely yours, without the subtle influence of a notification, a search suggestion or an ad that seemed to know what you were thinking?

## in-005
This is not just "phone addiction." It is what the hacker Kevin Mitnick, in <em>The Art of Deception</em> (2002), called <strong>social engineering</strong>: getting people to act, or to hand over information, by exploiting our tendency to trust and to follow the easiest path. Mitnick was talking about scams against companies; the platforms turned the same logic into an industrial process: test, measure and repeat billions of times.

## in-006
The result is your <strong>Data Double</strong>: a statistical version of you, made of billions of fragments, that circulates among companies independently of you (the term comes from the sociology of surveillance: Kevin Haggerty and Richard Ericson, 2000). For the market, that version is usually worth more, and is easier to use, than the flesh-and-blood person.

## in-007
In paraphrase, the thesis of <strong>Shoshana Zuboff</strong> in <em>The Age of Surveillance Capitalism</em> (2019): human experience became free raw material, extracted in order to predict and sell our future behavior. It is a silent rupture, because it was designed not to be noticed.

## in-008
A Case: When the Feed Became an Experiment

## in-009
In January 2012, for one week, Facebook changed what 689,003 people saw in their news feed: for some, fewer positive posts; for others, fewer negative ones. Nobody was told. The researchers wanted to know whether mood spreads through a network, and concluded that it does: people who saw fewer positive posts wrote, on average, slightly fewer positive words (Kramer, Guillory and Hancock, <em>PNAS</em>, 2014).

## in-010
The most honest part of this story is the size of the effect: <strong>tiny</strong>. Nobody had their mood hijacked. The scandal was something else: a company tested emotions on hundreds of thousands of people, without asking permission, and we only found out because it published the result. How much of this happens without turning into a paper? That is the question this book carries.

## in-011
Another case, more everyday: in 2012, the <em>New York Times</em> showed how Target used about 25 purchased products to give each customer a score for the likelihood of pregnancy. The most repeated version (a father who finds out about his daughter's pregnancy through the coupons) is disputed and has never been independently confirmed; what is documented is the model. That is enough for the lesson: your shopping routine says more about you than you imagine.

The Autopsy of Control

## in-013
How did we get here without noticing? Modern technology is not neutral; it is designed to collect. Four fronts, each with a dated fact:

## in-014
<span class="card-title">01. The Pocket Tracker</span> Your smartphone carries an accelerometer, a gyroscope and GPS, sensors that reveal intimate habits. In 2019, a <em>New York Times</em> investigation analyzed the location of more than 12 million phones in the US and recognized people by their routes.

## in-015
<span class="card-title">02. The Panoptic Home</span> Voice assistants and home cameras turn the house into a data source. In 2023, the US consumer protection agency (the FTC) fined Amazon 25 million dollars for keeping children's Alexa voice recordings for longer than it should have.

## in-016
<span class="card-title">03. Surveilled Money</span> Every digital payment leaves a trail, and the trail can become leverage. In 2022, Canada ordered the freezing of accounts linked to the truckers' protests, without a prior court order.

## in-017
<span class="card-title">04. The All-Knowing Government</span> Data and algorithms in the hands of the state can err at scale. In the Netherlands, a fraud-detection system for childcare benefits wrongly accused thousands of families, especially of immigrant background, and the scandal brought down the government in 2021.

## in-018
The Escape Map

## in-019
If you feel a growing discomfort, a physical sense that something has broken in the human experience, you are not crazy: you are perceiving. What is at stake goes beyond abstract "privacy." It is <strong>Free Will</strong>: if your choices are predicted and influenced before they happen, are they still yours? One caveat: prediction is not destiny. Models get averages right; they do not decree what you will do tomorrow. But influencing, at every moment and at scale, is already enough to change what a society believes and buys.

## in-020
And we are not unarmed. We will see how <strong>Open Source Systems</strong> give transparency back; how <strong>Cryptography</strong> protects conversations and money, and why it is so controversial; how <strong>privacy laws</strong> raise barriers; and how <strong>Obfuscation</strong> (Finn Brunton and Helen Nissenbaum, 2015) lets you blend into the crowd, like the tactic's <strong>"Gray Man"</strong>: invisible not through absence, but through apparent irrelevance. We will claim <strong>neurorights</strong> and imagine, with Solarpunk, a technology in service of life and not only of profit.

## in-022
How to Read This Book: A Pact of Rigor

## in-023
This book combines four kinds of statement, and you deserve to know how to tell them apart: <strong>documented facts</strong> (with source and date), <strong>estimates</strong> (numbers that vary depending on who measures), <strong>hypotheses</strong> (plausible scenarios, flagged as such) and <strong>provocations</strong> (the author's voice). A book about manipulation cannot afford to manipulate.

## in-024
This is <strong>not a book against technology</strong>. The same pattern recognition that watches us also helps read medical scans and translate languages with few speakers. The question running through these pages is: <strong>who controls, who benefits, who can audit and who can say no</strong>.

## in-025
And it is an <strong>organism under construction</strong>: a revised edition from November 2026, with data that ages and questions we do not yet know how to ask. In the Epilogue, we discuss what it would mean to write this book with many hands, without ever closing it.
