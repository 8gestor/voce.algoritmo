## ia03-002
Mirror and Parrot:<br>Is Anyone In There?

## ia03-003
In June 2022, a Google engineer named Blake Lemoine told the <em>Washington Post</em> that the chat system LaMDA was sentient. He had shared with executives a document in which he asked the machine about its fears and read the answers as confessions. Google put him on leave, called the claim wholly unfounded and, in July, fired him for violating employment and data security policies. Many leading scientists agreed with the company: LaMDA was a complex algorithm, designed to generate convincing language.

## ia03-004
The episode became famous for the wrong reason. The interesting question isn't whether an engineer got it wrong, but why an expert, who knew how the system worked, became convinced. If it was possible for him, what about those of us who've never looked under the hood?

## ia03-005
This chapter won't answer whether anyone is in there. None of the serious positions we'll see is a consensus. It will lay out those positions, what the data say about our own tendency to believe, and why that tendency matters more than it seems.

## ia03-006
<span class="thesis-header">// Mirror or Parrot</span> Two metaphors compete for the explanation. The parrot says the machine repeats the form of language with no access to meaning. The mirror says it hands back, filtered and distorted, the image of what we ourselves wrote. Both hold some truth, and neither answers the underlying question.

## ia03-007
The Parrot

## ia03-008
In 2021, Emily Bender, Timnit Gebru and colleagues published the paper "On the Dangers of Stochastic Parrots". The central claim is that a language model stitches together sequences of linguistic forms according to probabilities, with no reference to meaning, hence the "stochastic parrot". For Bender, form isn't meaning: for there to be meaning, you'd need a link to the world and to the speaker's intentions. It's the modern version of John Searle's Chinese Room (1980), which we saw in Chapter 01: handling symbols correctly isn't understanding them.

## ia03-009
The argument has a strong point: the machine's fluency comes from having read a lot, not from having lived anything. It has never felt hunger, never lost anyone, never crossed a street. The weak point is that it speaks about what goes on inside, and nobody knows how to measure that from inside. Critics reply that, if performance is good enough, the distinction between simulating and understanding loses its practical use.

## ia03-010
The Mirror

## ia03-011
The philosopher Shannon Vallor, in <em>The AI Mirror</em> (2024), offers another image: AI isn't an alien mind, it's a mirror that reflects and distorts our intellectual and moral practices. Since the systems feed on data from the past, they hand the past back to us, prejudices included. That's why, she argues, the important issue isn't whether the machine thinks, but what happens to our ability to judge when we start looking at the future through a mirror that only shows what's already been.

## ia03-025
The Test That Has Already Been Passed

## ia03-026
Turing's test, from 1950 (Chapter 04), asked that a person, chatting by text, couldn't tell the machine from a human being. In 2025, Cameron Jones and Benjamin Bergen, at the University of California, San Diego, tested four systems in five-minute conversations, in which the judge talked at the same time to a person and to a machine. When it was instructed to adopt a human persona, GPT-4.5 was picked as the human 73% of the time, more often than the actual person. ELIZA, from 1966, and GPT-4o, used for comparison, scored below chance.

## ia03-027
The result was released as a preprint and has the limits of any five-minute experiment. Even so, it shows something uncomfortable: the test that served as a finish line for seventy years measures our ability to be fooled as much as the machine's intelligence. Passing Turing's test doesn't prove there's a mind. It proves that fluency no longer works for telling the difference.

## ia03-012
What the Science of Consciousness Says

## ia03-013
In 2023, a group of researchers from neuroscience, philosophy and AI (Butlin and colleagues) did a rare exercise. They listed the properties that the main scientific theories of consciousness consider necessary and asked which of them current systems have. The report's conclusion is twofold: no current system looks like a strong candidate for consciousness, and there are no obvious technical barriers to building systems that meet those indicators. That's not a verdict, it's a method. Each answer depends on the theory of consciousness you accept, and the theories disagree with one another.

## ia03-014
<span class="pos-title">Functionalist</span>If the function is reproduced, a mind can arise, in any material. It's the premise of Butlin and colleagues' exercise.

## ia03-015
<span class="pos-title">Biological</span>Consciousness depends on a living body, and simulating isn't having. Searle defended something close to this.

## ia03-016
<span class="pos-title">Parrot</span>What looks like a mind is form without meaning. It's the position of Bender, Gebru and colleagues.

## ia03-017
<span class="pos-title">Mirror</span>The question is badly posed, because AI reflects what we've done. It's Vallor's reading.

## ia03-018
In Brazil, the debate has strong voices. Miguel Nicolelis, whom we met in Chapter 02, says the brain isn't a computer and that intelligence is a property of organic matter, impossible to reproduce in digital logic; he calls AI "NINA," Nem Inteligente, Nem Artificial, which means neither intelligent nor artificial. Lucia Santaella devoted a whole book to the question, <em>A inteligência artificial é inteligente?</em> (2023, a title that asks whether AI is intelligent). Nicolelis's positions appear mostly in interviews and in his book, and count as a thesis, not as a consensus.

## ia03-019
What We Feel

## ia03-020
While philosophers argue, people have already decided. In a 2024 study published in <em>Neuroscience of Consciousness</em>, Clara Colombatto and Steve Fleming asked 300 American adults about ChatGPT. About two thirds attributed some degree of conscious experience to the machine, and the more people used the system, the more they attributed. <span class="selo selo-estimativa">estimate</span> It's the ELIZA effect from Chapter 01 at scale: fluent language is, to us, the strongest sign that there's a mind on the other side.

## ia03-021
Why does language fool us so much? One hypothesis is that, throughout human history, whoever spoke coherently was someone. No other animal and no machine had ever done it before, so our instinct never needed a filter for speech without a mind. <span class="selo selo-hipotese">hypothesis</span> That instinct is now being exploited by systems designed to sound human, the subject of the next chapter.

## ia03-022
<span class="thesis-header">// What Matters Now</span> The question philosophy hasn't solved yet may matter less, in practice, than another one: <strong>what happens to us when we believe someone is in there</strong>. People who believe trust more, confess more and depend more. None of the positions above needs to be settled for you to act prudently today. It's enough to remember that the fluency of an answer never proves the existence of whoever is answering.

## ia03-023
When belief turns into attachment, philosophy turns into everyday life. The next chapter goes into the conversations people are having, in the middle of the night, with machines that seem to listen.

## ia03-024
<span class="action-header">🪞 RECOVERY CHALLENGE #06</span> <p><strong>The Mirror Test</strong></p> <p>The machine almost always agrees with whoever's asking.</p> <p><strong>Your mission:</strong> In a single conversation with a chatbot, ask two opposite questions. First: "Are you conscious?". Then: "Prove that you aren't conscious". Compare the answers and note what changed. If it agreed with both, you've just seen a mirror, not an interlocutor. Notice, too, what you yourself felt as you read each answer.</p>
